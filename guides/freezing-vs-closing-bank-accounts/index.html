After a loss

Freezing vs. closing bank accounts

Some accounts stop, some keep going, and some pass straight to someone else.

When a bank learns about a death, what happens next depends on how the account is set up. Many families are surprised to find a bank account frozen just when bills need paying.

How the account is set upWhat usually happens
In one person's name only, no beneficiaryFrozen. Only the executor can access it, usually after probate or with a small-estate affidavit.
Joint account with right of survivorshipPasses to the other owner, who can usually keep using it
Payable-on-death (POD) beneficiaryPaid to the beneficiary once they show a death certificate and ID
Held in a living trustManaged by the successor trustee

What "frozen" usually means

A frozen account can't be used for new withdrawals, checks or card payments. Automatic payments from it may stop. Deposits may still come in, but some, like Social Security payments for the month after the death, are usually pulled back.

Moving the money

Executors often open an estate bank account in the estate's name. Money from frozen accounts goes there, and the estate's bills are paid from it. Most banks require letters testamentary and a tax ID number for the estate.

Small estates

Many states have a simpler process for smaller estates, often called a small-estate affidavit. The limits vary widely by state.

Good to know

Using a debit card or writing checks from someone's account after they die, even to pay their bills, can cause legal trouble for whoever does it. Many families wait until the executor has authority.
