Skip probate

Living trusts: how they work and what they cost

A way to skip probate for almost everything, if it's set up all the way.

A living trust is a legal document that holds someone's property while they're alive and says who gets it afterward. It's called "living" because it's set up during life, not by a will after death.

The three roles

RoleWho it usually isWhat they do
GrantorThe person making the trustPuts property into it and sets the rules
TrusteeUsually the same person, while aliveManages the property, just as they did before
Successor trusteeA family member, friend or professionalTakes over at death or if the grantor can't manage, then follows the trust's instructions

Most living trusts are revocable, meaning the person who made it can change it or cancel it at any time. Day to day, nothing much changes: they still live in the house, use the accounts and file taxes the same way.

What makes it official

A living trust takes effect when the person making it signs it. In most states the trust itself is legally valid without a notary, though a few ask for one or for witnesses. In practice, banks, title companies and county recorders usually want to see a notarized signature before they'll work with the trust, so almost everyone has it notarized.

Unlike a will, a trust usually isn't filed with a court or any government office. The signed original is kept somewhere safe, often with the will, and the successor trustee needs to know where it is.

Signing makes the trust real, but on its own it's an empty container. It only controls property once things are moved into it.

Why families use one

  • Whatever is in the trust skips probate, so the successor trustee can usually act in weeks rather than waiting on a court.
  • It stays private. A will that goes through probate generally becomes a public court record; a trust usually doesn't.
  • It covers incapacity. If the person can no longer manage their affairs, the successor trustee can step in without a court-appointed guardian.
  • It can handle homes in more than one state without a separate probate case in each.

What it doesn't do

A revocable living trust generally doesn't reduce income tax or estate tax, and it doesn't usually protect assets from creditors or long-term care costs. Families who want those things typically look at other kinds of trusts, with a lawyer.

Most people with a trust still have a short will, often called a pour-over will. It catches anything that was never put into the trust and sends it there, though those items may still go through probate first.

What it costs

A trust usually costs more to set up than a simple will, whether through a lawyer or an online service. Prices vary widely by state and by how complicated things are. Many families weigh that cost against what probate would cost and how long it takes where they live, which also varies a lot by state.

Good to know

A trust only controls what's actually in it. Paperwork that's signed but never followed through, so the house and accounts stay in the person's own name, is one of the most common reasons a trust doesn't do its job.
